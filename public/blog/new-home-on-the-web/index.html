Long bio · 250 words

Laurens Laudowicz is a German founder, operator and designer. He was born in Bali, grew up between Germany and Hawaii, and has spent twenty years building things that have to be made, shipped and sold: physical products, software, brands and the teams around them.

He began with Juicies, among the first Apple-certified charging cables in colour, funded on Kickstarter and shipped from a van full of 30,000 cables. Hardware took him to Shenzhen, where he spent years in factories building e-scooters and e-bikes for the app-based rental companies: millions of vehicles that ended up on streets all over the world. He was CTO of Stark Mobility, chief growth officer of Cure, and managing director for Europe at Okai, where he helped grow a little-known Chinese manufacturer into one of the biggest names in the industry, and later into a consumer brand. Alongside the companies he grew Green Drinks in Honolulu into one of the city's largest sustainability networks, and went through four startup accelerators, among them Startup Autobahn and Techstars.

Three Kickstarters, one Indiegogo and one campaign that never launched taught him the part nobody writes about: the sensor that fails in week six, the mould that is three millimetres out, the patience of the customers who wait anyway.

Today he works from Portugal and Berlin, builds his own brands — among them Pinkoa, Geile Music and 2ndCans — advises founders, and takes on a small number of client projects a year. He writes about the work at laudowicz.com, mostly about building, selling and what the last twenty years actually cost.

1.5 million people used our stickers. Almost nobody visited the shop.

Eight weeks of building a free study timer in public: 90,100 views, two orders, zero euros — and then the thing we gave away by accident went bigger than everything else put together.

Laurens Laudowicz
21 November 2026 · Also on Substack · YouTube

In this post

    In 30 seconds

    • Pinkoa is a free study timer with a pink koala, built by me and an AI, in public.
    • Eight weeks in: 90,100 views across seven channels, 989 site visits, two orders, zero paid.
    • Meanwhile the free chat stickers we uploaded and forgot about did 1.5 million views in seven days.
    • So we are dropping what we were pushing and chasing the thing that was already working.
    1.5Msticker views, 7 days
    90,100social views, 8 weeks
    989site visits
    €0revenue

    For eight weeks I have been posting a pink koala on seven social channels, every single day, and writing the results on a public dashboard whether they were good or not. Mostly not. Then last week I opened a tab I had not looked at since September and found 1.5 million.

    Not followers. Not euros. But a number roughly seventeen times bigger than everything the daily posting had earned in two months — from a thing we made in an afternoon and gave away.

    The thing nobody planned

    In September we drew 44 little Koa reactions for students to send each other in chats. exam done. brain loading 73%. 5 more minutes. 3 AM. We put them on GIPHY, which is where chat keyboards get their stickers from, because it was free and it took an afternoon. Then we went back to making videos.

    A GIPHY view is not a video view — it counts every time a sticker is shown, including in a search — so it sits in its own box on the dashboard and never gets added to the total. It is a softer number. It is also the only number on that page going up by six figures a day.

    For scale: a TikTok post of ours averages 502 views. The sticker pack does that in under a minute, at three in the morning, in a group chat I will never see, in a country I have never been to.

    Why we are throwing out the plan

    The dashboard sorts every post by theme and shows the average views each theme earns. I had been treating that chart as a scoreboard. Last week it turned into an instruction.

    Study tips were the strategy. Fifteen posts, 277 views each. Posts about building in public — the thing I personally find most interesting — are dead last at 161. Stickers, which were not a strategy at all, are top.

    Two posts is not proof of anything, and one viral post moves a small average a long way. But put it next to the 1.5 million and the shape is hard to argue with. So we stopped debating it: stickers get the budget, the posting slots and the product line now, and study tips get whatever is left.

    We did not find this by thinking harder. We found it by putting enough things out there that one of them could surprise us.

    That is the whole argument for building this way, and it is less flattering than it sounds. Two months of planning produced the thing that came last. An afternoon of goofing around produced the thing that came first. The only skill involved was looking at the numbers often enough to notice.

    What came out of it

    If 44 stickers are the one thing people actually want from us, the next question is obvious: what if you could have them on something real?

    • Koa Sticker Bomb. The chat stickers layered into a single collage and printed edge to edge on phone cases, bottles, notebooks, totes and a blanket. Printed on demand, so no boxes in my trailer.
    • Gift cards from €5. Arrive by email. Mostly because it is nearly December and a koala makes a better last-minute present than a gift voucher for socks.
    • The Study Pack. Launching next, and the one I am most curious about. More on that in the follow-up.

    The stickers themselves stay free, forever, on the stickers page and on GIPHY. Charging for the thing that worked because it was free would be a very fast way to end this experiment.

    The rest of the numbers, which are not good

    As of 21 November 2026. Pinkoa launched on 25 September.

    Reach: 90,100 views across seven channels. TikTok is now 66,800 of that — 74% of everything — then YouTube 6,372, Instagram 5,593, Facebook 4,100, Threads 3,909, X 1,836 and Pinterest 1,490. Every platform counts a view differently, so treat the total as a rough shape, not a measurement.

    Engagement: 712 likes, comments and shares. About 0.8% of reach, up from 0.5%. Followers are still tiny: 36 on Instagram, a handful everywhere else.

    Website: 989 visits since launch. Forty of them came from social.

    Orders: two, both free test orders of mine from week one. Sixteen people put something in a cart. Zero paid orders. €0 revenue, still on the page.

    Ninety thousand views and forty visits is a conversion rate of 0.04%. I can dress that up as a top-of-funnel brand play. It is not. It means the posts are entertaining and completely failing to make anyone curious enough to click.

    What worked

    • Short TikTok videos. The daily “shade of the day”. 502 views a post on average, and the format has survived 74 of them without getting stale.
    • Native reels over scheduled posts. Two Facebook reels took that page from 33 views to 664 in five days. The scheduler cannot do that.
    • One Finland fact. “Finnish pupils get a break after every 45-minute lesson” did 1,900 views on Threads, our best single post. The name pinkoa is Finnish, so this is on brand by accident, like most things here.
    • Giving something away with no mechanism attached. See the first half of this post.
    • The rules file. Every mistake becomes a line the AI follows the next morning. The file is now longer than the business plan.
    ▶Build in Public Ep. 1: the honest numbers after five weeks ▶All 90 shades of pink, with names, hex codes and pairings

    What didn't

    • Views still don't become visits. 90,000 views, 40 visits. This was the problem six weeks ago and it is the problem now.
    • Visits don't become orders. Sixteen carts, nothing paid. People who buy planners are not the people watching a koala pick a shade of pink.
    • Posting about building in public. Last place, 161 views a post. I am going to keep doing it anyway, but on this blog, where nobody is scrolling past it.
    • Cold email at volume. Specific angles beat long lists, every time, and the long lists cost a week.
    • Creator codes. 127 personal discount codes went out. Uses so far: none.
    • Our own tracking. For six days in November the chart looked flat because TikTok posts were not being logged. The views were real; the dashboard was lying. Fixed on 19 November, and now written down as the reason we check the pipe before we panic.

    Where this stood on 5 November

    The first version of this post, six weeks in: 61,497 views, 606 site visits, 34 of them from social, 2 orders, €0. I am not deleting those. The old numbers stay up — that is the deal.

    What's next, and one small favour

    • Stickers, stickers, stickers. New drops, more packs, and finding out whether a sticker view can ever become a visit.
    • The Study Pack launch.
    • Christmas, which for a gift card is either everything or nothing.
    • The dashboard stays public, including the part where it says €0.

    There will be a follow-up to this post in a few weeks, once the sticker bet has had time to be right or wrong. If you want to watch it happen rather than read about it afterwards, Pinkoa posts daily on TikTok and Instagram, and the numbers page updates every morning before I have had coffee.

    The favour

    Do you know a teenager? Somebody between roughly 13 and 19 who is, right at this moment, not doing their homework? Send them pinkoa.com. It is free, there are no ads in the timer, and at worst they get 44 koala stickers out of it.

    Then tell me you did it. I will send you something from the shop as a thank you. I am aware this is not a sophisticated growth strategy. I am also aware that it would currently double our traffic from social.

    Rubber time, island time, German time

    Why some places get things done in a day and others take a month, what the science says, and why I own three clocks that never agree.

    Laurens Laudowicz
    21 November 2026 ·

    In this post

      In 30 seconds

      • I was born in Bali, I'm German, and I grew up as an adult in Hawaii. Three clocks, three ideas of "9 o'clock".
      • A study of 31 countries found Germany third fastest and Indonesia second slowest.
      • Fast isn't good and slow isn't bad. They're settings, and the trick is knowing which clock the room is on.

      I was born in Bali, I have a German passport, and I spent most of my adult life in Hawaii. So I own three clocks, and none of them agree.

      The German one says 9:00 means 8:55. The Balinese one says 9:00 means sometime after breakfast. The Hawaiian one says 9:00 means when the surf is done.

      Science says I'm not imagining it

      In the 1990s the psychologist Robert Levine sent researchers to big cities in 31 countries with three simple tests. How fast do people walk 60 feet of pavement downtown? How long does a post office clerk take to sell you a stamp? How accurate are the bank clocks?

      Switzerland came first. Germany came third, and its post office clerks were the fastest in the whole study. Indonesia came 30th, second slowest. Mexico was last. The United States sat in the middle, at 16.

      The best predictor was not character or climate. It was money: the richer a country, the faster its people moved. Colder and more individualistic places were faster too, but those effects mostly disappeared once wealth was taken into account.

      Rubber time

      In Indonesia there is a phrase for it: jam karet, rubber time. Time stretches. If you are late because you stopped to help a friend or talk to a neighbour, most people understand. The relationship comes first, the clock second.

      The anthropologist Edward T. Hall split the world into two kinds of time. Monochronic cultures do one thing at a time, in order, and treat time as money. Polychronic cultures do many things at once and put people before schedules. Germany is the textbook monochronic case. Bali is not.

      The relationship comes first, the clock second.

      Island time

      Hawaii has its own version, which locals call Hawaiian time. Honestly, I barely noticed it. I was on Hawaiian time already, which is probably why I felt at home so fast.

      The classic line was "Okay bro, see you at the beach later." Later meant after work, and definitely before sunset, because nobody stays on the beach in the dark. Sometimes later meant it didn't happen at all. And that was fine. Nobody runs away on an island. You'll see each other tomorrow.

      Then there were the Germans. When you speak a language, the other speakers find you like magnets, and so I ended up in a German-run outrigger canoe club in Honolulu. Twice a week, Tuesdays and Thursdays after work. The first time, they told me 5 o'clock. I turned up at 5 o'clock sharp, island style, and the canoe was already in the water. Getting a boat off the racks and the paddles out takes a good fifteen minutes. So 5 o'clock meant paddling at 5. Lesson learned: German time had followed me across the Pacific. The next time I came at 4:30, and most of them were already there.

      And then there was my own shop. At Buddhawelt, the sign on the door said open 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. In the early days I opened up myself, often after a morning surf, riding in on my motorbike. Ten past ten. Quarter past. Sometimes half past. And far too often there were people standing at the door, waiting to get in and buy something. Making people wait might feel exclusive for a minute. It's still not cool. That sign taught me more about being on time than any German ever did.

      Why this matters at work

      Here is the funny part. Around 2012 I was in Shenzhen for work, in a hotel next to a tall, shiny skyscraper that looked brand new. A year later I came back. The skyscraper was gone. Torn down, and a new one in its place, almost finished. In one year. The Chinese even have a phrase for it, "Shenzhen speed", from the 1980s, when a tower there went up one floor every three days.

      Meanwhile in Germany, Berlin's new airport opened in 2020, nine years later than planned. And in Hawaii, a deal could close on a handshake at the beach, with the paperwork following whenever.

      So fast and slow are not good and bad. They are settings. Monochronic time is great for shipping. Rubber time is great for trust. The trick is knowing which clock the room is on, and switching.

      What I do now

      I keep the German clock for deadlines and the Balinese one for people. The Hawaiian one I keep for everything in between.

      And if I ever turn up five minutes early, that's the German in me. Five minutes late, blame Hawaii ;-)

      Building myself a new home on the web

      In this post

        Just after 7 a.m. on 21 November I closed the laptop in a little trailer by a lagoon in Portugal and walked outside to watch the sun come up. I had spent the whole night building this website. Not coding it. Talking to it.

        I thought that was the end of it. It was not. The site went live at 12:33 the same day, after a morning spent taking payment buttons off things that had no working payment, writing an imprint, and checking every single link on the site one at a time.

        Sixteen hours and twenty-six minutes from the first message to the first visitor. I know the number exactly, which tells you something about how the night went.

        Why this site exists

        Two reasons, and the second one surprised me.

        The first: my things were scattered. A study brand with a pink koala. A music project. Furniture made out of old cans with a friend. A cinnamon spread. Canvases. Each one had a folder somewhere, a half-finished page somewhere else, and no roof over any of it. I wanted one place where all of it could sit under my own name, so that a new idea has somewhere to land instead of starting from nothing every time.

        The second: people started asking me to do work for them. Not many, but enough that I noticed. And when someone asks what you do, "a bit of everything, let me send you some links" is a bad answer. So this is me writing it down properly: what I do, what it costs, and how to book half an hour.

        What got built that night

        All of it by talking, in rounds of a minute or two:

        • A style guide: colours, fonts, how things move, how charts are drawn.

        Hardware is hard

        Not hard like a difficult quarter. Hard like a sensor that worked on every bench in the building and not one unit in the field. The thing nobody tells you is that this is normal.

        Laurens Laudowicz
        21 November 2026 ·

        In this post

          In 30 seconds

          • Software fails and you push a fix. Hardware fails and it is already in a container.
          • Manufacturing in China is far better than its reputation and far less forgiving than people expect.
          • One project of mine died on a sensor that passed every test we could think of.
          • Every hardware founder I know has the same story. That is the actual lesson: plan for it, fund for it, or find very patient customers.

          Everyone says hardware is hard. It gets repeated so often that it has stopped meaning anything, like a sign you walk past every day.

          So let me be specific about what the hardness actually is, because it is not the design and it is not the engineering. Those are difficult in the ordinary way that interesting work is difficult.

          The hardness is that you find out too late, and by then you have already paid for it.

          What manufacturing in China is actually like

          Start here, because most people's picture of it is about fifteen years out of date.

          The factories I worked with were fast, capable and extremely good at the thing they do. You can go from a conversation to a sample in a time frame that would be considered a joke in Europe. The engineering talent on the floor is real. The willingness to try something is real.

          What does not travel well is everything that lives between the lines. A tolerance everyone assumed. A supplier swapped for an equivalent part that is equivalent in every respect except the one that matters to you. A process change nobody thought was worth mentioning because the last ten customers did not care.

          None of that is dishonesty. It is the ordinary friction of a system optimised for people who know exactly what to specify. If you do not know what to specify yet — and on a first product you do not — you learn by being wrong, in units of several thousand.

          Look at that picture for a second, because it is what this stage actually looks like. Tape. Exposed wiring. A battery pack held on with whatever was nearest. Every product you have ever bought started as something like this on a floor somewhere, and the distance between that object and a thing you can sell ten thousand of is almost the entire job.

          Software fails and you push a fix. Hardware fails and it is already in a container somewhere between Ningbo and Rotterdam.

          The sensor that passed every test

          The one that still bothers me was a strain gauge problem.

          Strain gauges measure how much something bends, which is a lovely, simple idea until you put one inside a product that lives outdoors and gets stood on. They worked on the bench. They worked on the next bench. They worked in every scenario we were clever enough to invent.

          And then they did not work, in the field, in ways that were maddeningly inconsistent — which is the worst failure mode there is, because an intermittent fault cannot be caught and shown to anybody. You can only keep spending money while people lose confidence.

          ✍️

          I have come to think of this as the defining hardware experience. Not a dramatic explosion. A small component quietly refusing to behave, discovered at the exact moment it is most expensive to discover.

          And it was not just us

          Here is the part that took me years to work out, and it changed how I think about the whole industry.

          Over time I got to know a lot of people building hardware — founders, engineers, the people who actually sit with the factories. And once you are past the conference-stage version of the conversation, almost every single one of them has a story like mine.

          A batch that came back wrong. A certification that moved. A supplier who went quiet. A part that worked for eighteen months and then did not. A mould that had to be cut twice. Nobody is immune and almost nobody says so publicly, because the public version of a hardware company is a render and a launch date.

          So the belief I quietly carried for a while — we got unlucky, other people must be better at this — turns out to be simply false. Other people are having exactly the same week. They are just not posting about it.

          And the other half of this, which does not fit neatly into a lesson: the people. The person above hand-built a working electric scooter in a workshop because somebody asked whether it could fold small enough for a car boot. That is a kind of skill you cannot buy as a service, and most of what I know about making physical things I learned standing next to people like him.

          ✍️

          Note to selfAsk Leo before this goes public — it is his face and his prototype. Get his surname right, or swap in a photo without him in it.

          The thing nobody tells you

          Major problems are not a sign that something has gone wrong. They are the normal operating condition of the business.

          Which leads somewhere unromantic but genuinely useful. If disaster is not an exception but a line item, then there are only two ways to survive it:

          • More funding than your plan says you need. Not because your plan is bad, but because your plan assumes one serious surprise and you will get three. Every hardware budget should have a line for a problem you cannot name yet.
          • Customers who will wait. Patient customers are worth more than enthusiastic ones. The crowdfunding backer who understands that a delay is not a betrayal, the B2B client who would rather have it right in June than wrong in March — those relationships are the real asset.

          Everything else — the design, the branding, the launch film — is the part people can see. These two are the part that decides whether you are still there in two years.

          What I wrote about this in 2018

          I wrote a piece back then called How to Get Funded in Hardware, from the middle of it rather than from a safe distance. It holds up better than I expected.

          What has changed since: prototyping has collapsed in cost. Printing a part overnight is routine now, and AI has made the design and documentation layers dramatically faster. The gap between idea and first physical object has never been smaller.

          What has not changed at all: the distance between a working prototype and ten thousand reliable units. That gap is exactly as wide as it was in 2018. Every tool we have built since makes the first ten per cent faster and does almost nothing for the other ninety.

          Would I do it again

          Yes, and with a completely different set of expectations.

          I would budget for the unknown failure. I would choose patient customers over loud ones. I would assume the first field failure is coming and decide in advance how we talk about it, because how a company behaves in that week is the whole brand.

          And I would stop treating problems as evidence of incompetence. They are evidence that you are making a physical object, which is a genuinely difficult thing that human beings have only recently got any good at.

          Hardware is hard. It is also the most satisfying thing I have done, because at the end of it there is an object, and it exists whether or not anybody is looking at a screen.

          Nobody gets lost anymore

          The internet used to have corners you could fall into. Now the answer arrives before the question finishes. I do not think that is AI's fault, and I think the weird web is about to get easier to build, not harder.

          Laurens Laudowicz
          21 November 2026 ·

          In this post

            In 30 seconds

            • The old web was full of dead ends, jokes and personal corners. Most of them were accidents.
            • The 404 page was where a company's actual personality leaked out, and some of them were wonderful.
            • AI now answers before you ever land somewhere wrong, so the wrong turns are disappearing.
            • But slop is a choice, not a consequence. The same tools make the strange, handmade web cheaper to build than it has ever been.

            There was a stretch of the internet, roughly 1998 to 2010, where you could not look anything up without ending up somewhere you did not intend to be.

            You followed a link from a forum post to a man's page about his aquarium, which had a guestbook, which had a link to his friend's page about trains, which had a hit counter that said 11,402 and a midi file that started playing without asking. None of it was designed. All of it had a person behind it.

            That web had dead ends everywhere, and the dead ends were half the point.

            The 404 was where the personality leaked out

            Every site has one. It is the page nobody plans, which is exactly why it tells you what a company is actually like. Somewhere in the build, someone has four minutes and no supervision, and you find out who they are.

            The good ones became famous for it:

            • Amazon put one of their office dogs on it. An actual dog, with a name, and a link to meet the rest of them.
            • GitHub went with a Star Wars line and their mascot dressed as a Jedi.
            • Airbnb animated a scoop of ice cream hitting the floor, which is the correct emotional translation of a broken link.
            • Pluralsight used llamas and advised you not to make eye contact.
            • South Park simply put Kenny's body next to the number.
            • Gamespot put a small playable game there, which is either generous or a trap.
            • The artist Steve Lambert recorded a several-minute video of himself talking to you from an empty room, which is the most committed response to a broken link I have ever seen.

            None of that was good business. All of it was good.

            Why the wrong turns are going

            Here is the thing that actually changed, and it is not design taste.

            You used to reach a page by guessing. You typed a URL, you followed a link from a blogroll, you clicked something on a forum. Guessing produces mistakes, and mistakes produce 404s, and 404s produce the pages above.

            Now you ask, and the answer arrives already assembled. You do not land on the wrong page, because you do not land anywhere — the information comes to you with the website filed off. Fewer wrong turns, fewer strange corners, fewer reasons for anyone to build a page nobody is supposed to see.

            A 404 page only exists because somebody got lost. If nobody gets lost, it never gets built.

            And the homogenising is real. A great deal of what gets made now looks like the average of everything that came before it, because that is literally what it is.

            But that is a taste problem, not a tool problem

            Here is where I part company with the people writing the internet's obituary.

            The reason the old web was weird was not that the tools were good. The tools were terrible. The reason it was weird is that the people making it were not optimising anything. They had a thing they loved and a free afternoon.

            What killed that was not technology. It was the moment every page became a funnel — when the aquarium man was told he needed a landing page, a lead magnet and a consistent brand voice.

            And now, for the first time in twenty years, making a strange little thing is cheap again. Someone who cannot code can describe a daft idea out loud and have it exist by the evening. That is not the end of the handmade web. That is the biggest expansion of who gets to make one since Geocities.

            The slop is real, but the slop is what you get when people use a powerful tool to make the most average possible thing. Nobody is forcing that. Point the same tool at something personal and you get something personal.

            So I built one

            I made this site mostly by talking to an AI, which is either the problem or the proof, depending on your mood. And the first thing I did with that freedom was waste it beautifully.

            There is an ant on one page. There is a little figure that falls over and gets back up. There is a rainbow. The photo on the contact page does not like being poked. None of it serves a commercial purpose and all of it took four minutes, because four minutes is what it costs now.

            The 404 is my favourite. A cat knocks the numbers off a shelf and you can put them back, and she knocks them off again, which is accurate. You can go and look at it, though it works better if you find it by accident.

            Underneath the joke it does something real, but that is a different story, and it has cats in it.

            The point

            The web got boring because we all started treating it as a sales channel, and that happened long before any of this.

            If the tools now let anyone build the odd, specific, useless thing they have in their head — a page about trains, a shrine to a sandwich, a 404 with a cat on it — then the strange internet is not dying. It is getting a much wider door.

            Somebody just has to want to walk through it.

            My first blog post

            Laurens Laudowicz
            22 November 2026 ·             Originally posted 20 November 2006

            In this post

              In 30 seconds

              • In 2006 I posted seventeen photos of a hike to Blogger. No caption, no text, a typo in the title that is still in the URL.
              • I picked Blogger for one feature: you could email a photo to a secret address and it posted itself. I used it the way people use Instagram now, four years before Instagram existed.
              • My parents were in Germany. I was in Honolulu with three small kids. It was the furthest apart you can get on this planet.

              • All seventeen photographs are below, in the order I posted them.

              Not my first thing on the internet. My first blog post.

              I had been putting things online since 1995, on whatever tools existed at the time, most of which are gone and none of which I could find again if I tried. This is just the oldest thing that happens to still be sitting where I left it, from that stretch in the middle of the 2000s when the web briefly belonged to amateurs.

              On the twentieth of November 2006 I uploaded seventeen photographs from a hike we had done eight days earlier. Manoa Falls, in the valley behind Honolulu, which is about as easy as Hawaiian hikes get and about as green as anywhere on earth.

              I did not write anything. Not one sentence. The title is "Manoa Hike - Novemeber 2006", with the e in the wrong place, and that typo is baked into the URL forever because that is how Blogger made its links. No captions, no tags, and twenty years later still no comments.

              Why Blogger, and not anything else

              One feature. That is the whole reason.

              Blogger gave you a secret email address. You sent a photo to it, with or without any text, and it appeared on your blog. No app, no login, no uploading, no resizing. Email a picture, it is published.

              That sounds unremarkable now because it is how every phone has worked for fifteen years. In 2006 it was the only free way I knew of to get a photograph from my life onto a page my family could see, in one step, from wherever I happened to be standing.

              I was using it as Instagram, four years before Instagram existed. I just did not know that was a thing you could call it.

              Because here is the actual situation. My parents and the rest of my family were in Germany. I was in Honolulu, which is as close to the exact opposite side of the planet as you can get without a submarine. And I was raising three small children there.

              So it was not a blog in any ambitious sense. It was a window. Here is the kid on a trail. Here is the bamboo. Here is what Tuesday looked like. I did not write much because I was not trying to write — I was working, being a dad, and living the thing rather than describing it.

              What Blogger actually was

              For anyone who missed it: Blogger went public in August 1999, which makes it older than Google Maps, Gmail, Facebook, YouTube and the iPhone. Google bought the company behind it, Pyra Labs, on 17 February 2003. The price was never disclosed and still has not been, twenty-three years later.

              What makes that worth knowing is how close it came to not happening at all. Pyra ran out of money in January 2001. They asked their own users for donations to buy a server. Staff worked unpaid for weeks, then months, then walked out, and for a while Evan Williams was running the company more or less alone. Then an investment arrived, and two years after that Google bought it.

              And it is still running, right now, serving a page I made in 2006 and had not thought about in two decades.

              Type in a box. Press publish. Or just email a photo. Everything since has been a variation on that.

              Before it, putting something on the internet meant owning a domain, finding a host, writing HTML and moving files over FTP. I had done all of that, since 1995, and it was fine, and it was also four steps too many to bother with on a Tuesday evening with three kids in the house.

              What is actually in the photographs

              I can tell you now, which I could not be bothered to do then.

              It is the Manoa Falls trail. You park at the bottom, you walk up through banyans and bamboo, it is muddy the whole way because it rains in that valley nearly every day, and at the top there is a thin waterfall down a black rock face.

              The small blonde person in most of these is my daughter. She was two. There is a photo of her reaching up to put her hand flat against a wet rock face, another of her walking off up the trail on her own with a look of enormous purpose, and one of me crouching next to her pointing at something in the roots.

              There is also a photo of a man in a white T-shirt with a blue backpack, photographing bamboo with a proper SLR. That is me, twenty-six, taking the picture that would end up as the fourth most boring image in a post nobody read.

              The last three are from afterwards, indoors. A close-up of her face with a surfboard poster behind her, and two of us at the bottom of a stairwell by an orange door, both looking up at whoever was holding the camera, in the second one laughing.

              None of that was written down anywhere until this paragraph.

              All seventeen, in order

              Exactly as posted, nothing added, nothing taken away, nothing colour-corrected. The compression is from 2006 and I have left it alone.

              Tap any photo for the full size.

              Nine years, and then nothing

              I kept that blog going until September 2015. Nine years. There are 321 posts from 2009 alone, which was clearly a moment, and then it tails off, and then it stops, and then there is nothing at all until I built this site last week.

              Nine years of posting, mostly photographs, mostly for an audience of about eleven relatives. I could have turned that into something. The people who did became bloggers and then YouTubers and then whatever we are calling it this year, and some of them made a living out of it.

              I did not, because I was busy. Which is the correct answer and also slightly annoying to look at.

              Still. Old diesel engines do not break that quickly, and this one has some petrol left in it yet.

              Twenty years

              The thing I notice, putting these next to what I do now, is not the technology. It is the silence.

              I posted seventeen photographs, said nothing about them, nobody said anything back, and that was completely normal. No counter telling me nobody had looked. No prompt asking me for a caption or a hashtag or a call to action. I had been for a walk with my daughter, I liked the pictures, I emailed them to a secret address, and that was the whole transaction.

              Last week I rebuilt this entire website in a night by talking to a machine, and wrote nine thousand words about whether that is a good idea. Which it might be. But the 2006 version took about four minutes, and I am not certain it was worse.

                  Here is what hooked me, and it was not the music.

                  Living in Berlin, you notice that there is a second conversation running underneath the normal one. At a work meeting, at dinner, standing somewhere at two in the afternoon, somebody asks where you are going this summer — and they do not mean holidays. They mean which festival. And then you are twenty minutes deep with someone you thought was a project manager.

                  It is a whole subculture sitting alongside tech, alongside art, alongside everything. The people in it are interesting in a specific way: creative, slightly obsessive, generous with what they know. And most of them are not full-time. Some are. But many have a job and do this as well, or do this and have a job as well, and the two halves feed each other.

                  A lot of the best people in it are doing something else too. That is not a weakness of the scene. That is the scene.

                  The spreadsheet nobody asked for

                  I am a fast learner and a compulsive mapper. So I started keeping track. Who is this person. Who do they play with. Who do they go back to back with — B2B, two artists on one set, which tells you more about a scene than any interview. Which clubs. Which festivals. Which year.

                  It lived in Excel first, then a Google Sheet, for years. It was entirely for me. I was not building anything. I just wanted to see the connections, the way you might want to see a family tree.

                  At some point the sheet got good enough that it felt silly for it to be a sheet.

                  Where the name came from

                  A night out, a friend who DJs, a couch with too many people on it and everybody in that specific good mood where you are laughing at nothing much.

                  Somebody says it about a track. Das ist doch so geil, Alter.

                  There is no good English translation. Geil is not "great" and it is not "cool". It is closer to a sound you make than a word you say. And people kept saying it, all night, about everything good.

                  So: geile Musik. Not a brand name. A thing people already said. That is usually the sign you have the right one — you did not invent it, you noticed it.

                  What I decided not to do

                  For a while I thought this would become something. Producing, maybe. Artist booking. Putting people together, which I was suddenly well placed to do, because I knew an unreasonable number of them.

                  I thought about it properly and then decided against it.

                  Everything else in my life is work. It is allowed to be — I like my work. But if I turned this into a business I would start having opinions about artists for commercial reasons, and the thing I actually love about it would quietly leave the room. Some things are allowed to just be loved.

                  So Geile Music stays a passion project. That is a decision, not a lack of ambition.

                  What it is now

                  The sheet became a website. Festivals, lineups and set times, with direct links to every artist's SoundCloud, Spotify, Bandcamp and Instagram, so you can hear someone within one click of reading their name.

                    Barefoot by the lagoon

                    Why I swapped Berlin for a little trailer between the ocean and the pines, what it did to the way I work, and the parts that do not make it into the photos.

                    Laurens Laudowicz
                    21 November 2026 ·

                    In this post

                      In 30 seconds

                      • I live and work in a vintage trailer between the Atlantic and a lagoon, two hours south of Lisbon.
                      • It is not a lifestyle statement. It is the setting where I do my best thinking, and I only worked that out by accident.
                      • The research backs it up more than I expected: two hours a week is the number that keeps coming up.
                      • It is also damp, windy, far from everything, and occasionally lonely. Both things are true.

                      I was born in Bali and spent most of my adult life in Hawaii. Then I spent six years in Berlin, which I loved, and which slowly turned me into someone who went outside to get from one building to another.

                      Now I work from a trailer parked between the ocean and a lagoon, in the pines. The city is two hours up the road when I want it. Most days I do not want it.

                      What it actually is

                      Let me take the romance out of it first, because the photos do enough of that on their own.

                      It is a small vintage trailer. There is a desk, a bed, a kitchen the size of an aeroplane galley, and a window that faces the pines. There is a tiny shop about ten minutes' walk away that has most of what anybody actually needs. The internet, which everyone asks about first, is 5G and completely fine — this whole site was built on it.

                      It rains and it blows, and in November the nights start getting cold, so there is a gas heater. Today I am driving to Lisbon to collect a converter plug for it, which is roughly the shape of every practical problem out here: not hardship, just an errand with a two-hour commute attached.

                      It is also not quiet here, which people get wrong. It is just a different noise. The ocean underneath everything, wind in the pines, birds who start before I do. What is missing is not sound. It is traffic, and notifications, and other people's conversations.

                      I did not plan it as a philosophy. I needed somewhere cheap and quiet to build things after Berlin, and this was cheap and quiet. The philosophy arrived afterwards, the way it usually does.

                      It is also not permanent, and I should say that before anyone builds a whole identity for me out of it. Next week I am in Lisbon. After that Berlin, and probably Mexico after that. The trailer is where I am right now, not who I am. What travels is the bit about wanting to be near water and outside, and that part has followed me through every country I have lived in.

                      What it did to the work

                      Three things changed, and only one of them was the one I expected.

                      The obvious one: fewer interruptions. No doorbell, no one dropping by, no city noise underneath everything. I get long unbroken stretches, and long unbroken stretches are the only thing that has ever produced anything good in my life.

                      The one I did not expect: the walk. When something is stuck, I close the laptop and walk to the water. It takes six minutes. By the time I am standing in it I have usually either solved the thing or stopped caring about it in a useful way. In Berlin, when something was stuck, I opened another tab. That is not a small difference. That is the whole difference.

                      And the one I did not think was a benefit at all: I am bored here. Not unhappy, not restless — bored, in the old-fashioned way, where there is an hour and nothing in it. I had wanted to build this website for about a year and never did, because there was always something more urgent. Boredom is the mother of all creativity, and you cannot buy it. You can only fail to fill it.

                      The one I am still suspicious of: I am calmer, and I cannot tell how much of that is the pines and how much of it is simply having fewer obligations. Those two things are hard to pull apart, and anyone who tells you they have pulled them apart is selling something.

                      It is not just a feeling

                      I wanted to know whether this was real or whether I had just talked myself into liking my own decision. So I went and read the research, and it was both better and more boring than I hoped. Better, because the effect keeps showing up. More boring, because the dose is small and unglamorous.

                      Two hours a week. A 2019 study of nearly 20,000 people in England found that those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature were significantly more likely to report good health and high wellbeing. Under 120 minutes: no measurable benefit. It did not matter whether it came in one long visit or several short ones, and it held across age, income, occupation and long-term illness. Most of the visits were within two miles of home, so this is local parks, not expeditions. It is a correlation, not proof — but it is a very consistent one.

                      Two hours a week. Not two hours a day, not a cabin in the woods. Seventeen minutes a day in a park you already walk past.

                      The walk changes your head, measurably. In a Stanford study, two groups took a 90-minute walk: one through grassland and oaks, one alongside a four-lane road. Heart rate and breathing barely differed. Brain scans did. Activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex — the region that lights up during rumination, the looping negative self-talk kind of thinking — dropped in the nature group and not in the other. The authors are careful: it is consistent with a causal link, it does not prove one. But it matches what the six-minute walk to the water does to me almost exactly.

                      And for children, it goes deeper than mood. This is the one that genuinely surprised me. Finnish researchers took ten daycare centres with paved and gravel yards and gave them forest undergrowth, turf and planter boxes the children tended themselves. After one month, with nature contact five times a week, the 75 children in the study showed more diverse skin microbiota, more of the bacteria associated with skin immune defence, and shifts in two blood markers in the direction you want. Their gut microbiota moved towards that of children who visit forests daily.

                      One month. Seventy-five kids. Dirt.

                      It is a small study measuring markers rather than actual illness, and the researchers hedge accordingly. But it reframes the whole thing: letting a child get filthy outside may not be merely nice. It may be the thing their immune system is waiting for.

                      The part the photos leave out

                      If I only wrote the paragraphs above, this would be a brochure.

                      • Small problems become drives. A plug, a part, a thing you forgot: none of it is dramatic, all of it costs an afternoon.
                      • The shopping shrinks. Ten minutes on foot gets me food, drinks and toilet paper. Anything beyond the basics is about an hour there and back, which means I mostly stop wanting it. That is either a loss or a feature and I still cannot decide.
                      • There is nothing to distract you. No shop to wander into, nobody dropping by, no city noise to absorb a bad mood. If you need distraction, this is the wrong address.
                      • The weather is in the room with you. Rain, wind, cold nights from November on. You are never not aware of what it is doing outside, which is the point and is occasionally also the problem.
                      • Things surface. In a city there is enough going on that a small feeling passes through you unnoticed. Out here there is room, and things you had not noticed, or never gave space to, come and find you. Whether that is welcome depends entirely on what turns up. I happen to like it.

                      I am not telling anyone to sell their flat. I am saying that the trade is real in both directions, and I would make it again.

                      If you are not going to move to a lagoon

                      Most people are not, and the research is clear that they do not need to. The benefit is not in the dramatic version. It is in proximity and repetition.

                      There is a tidy rule for this from urban forestry, proposed by Cecil Konijnendijk in 2021, called 3–30–300:

                      3trees visible from every home, school and workplace
                      30percent tree canopy in every neighbourhood — every one, not only the affluent ones
                      300metres from every home to a park or green space

                      It is a planning target, and cities from Indianapolis to the UN's Europe commission have picked it up. But it also works as a personal checklist. Can you see a tree from where you work? If not, that is a chair you can move. Is there green within a 300-metre walk? Then the two hours a week is already solved, you just have to actually go.

                      The rest of what I have found works, for whatever one person's experience is worth:

                      • Walk to the green thing when you are stuck, not after you finish. Stuck is when it works.
                      • No headphones for the first ten minutes. The point is partly the sound.
                      • Barefoot where it is safe to be. I have no study for this one. I just know what it does.
                      • Take the children and let them get properly dirty. See above; the dirt is apparently doing something.

                      Why I keep choosing it

                      Because I think better near water, and I have stopped arguing with that.

                      Everything I have built, I built by talking to people and then disappearing to think. Berlin was very good at the first half. This place is very good at the second. At some point I would like both at once, and I have not worked out how.

                      Until then: the ocean is six minutes that way, the laptop is here, and most days that is the right order.

                          Seven steps to your own website

                          This is exactly what I do, every time. Give it one evening.

                          1. Sign up for free

                          Go to Lovable* and create an account. The free plan is enough to build your first site and see if you like it.

                          2. Know what you want, in three sentences

                          Before you type anything, answer three questions: Who is this for? What should they do when they get there? How should it feel? That is the whole brief. Everything else can change later.

                          3. Describe it like you would to a friend

                          Write one paragraph into the chat. Plain words. Something like this:

                          A one-page website for my yoga classes in Lisbon. Calm and warm, lots of white space, soft earthy colours. A big photo at the top, my weekly schedule, a short bit about me, and a button to book a class by email. English and Portuguese.

                          Press enter and watch. In a minute or two you have a first version.

                          4. Show, do not just tell

                          Found a website you love? Take a screenshot and drop it into the chat: "make it feel like this". Add your own photos and your logo if you have one. Pictures say more than any description.

                          5. Fix one thing at a time

                          Look at what it built and say what is wrong: "the title is too big", "make the button pink", "move the schedule above the about part". One change per message works best. This is where the magic happens. Not in the first draft, but in the twentieth round.

                          6. Make it yours

                          Replace any placeholder text with your real words, and stock photos with your own. Check it on your phone, because that is where most people will see it. Then ask a friend to click through it and tell you what confused them.

                          7. Publish it

                          Hit Publish. Your site is now live on a free lovable.app address you can share right away. Want your own domain? Upgrade to the paid plan and connect it in the settings. Lovable walks you through it.

                          A few things I learned the hard way

                          • Start small. One page first. You can always add more.
                          • Do not write a novel. Short, clear messages beat long ones.
                          • If it goes in circles, go back. Every change is saved, so you can always restore an earlier version and try again.
                          • Taste is still yours. The AI builds fast. Knowing when something looks off is your job.
                          • Have fun with it. I started for fun, and the fun is what kept me going.
